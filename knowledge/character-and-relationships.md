# Characters and relationships

Use this module for character creation, arc planning, motivation repair, relationship movement, or speaker differentiation.

## Character engine

For every major character, distinguish:

- **external desire**: the result they actively pursue;
- **immediate need**: what they require in the current situation;
- **fear or wound**: what cost they organize their behavior to avoid;
- **false or incomplete belief**: the rule they use to interpret events;
- **resource and leverage**: what lets them act on the story;
- **moral line**: what they claim they will not do;
- **contradiction**: two values or desires that cannot both remain intact.

A biography is not an arc. An arc is a sequence of pressured choices whose consequences make the old behavior increasingly expensive.

## Arc construction

Build the arc as:

```text
baseline strategy
→ pressure exposes its limit
→ character doubles down or experiments
→ choice creates cost
→ evidence challenges belief
→ climactic choice confirms, revises, or corrupts the belief
→ new behavior has visible consequences
```

Flat arcs are valid when the character's stable belief changes the world or other people. Negative arcs still require intelligible choices, not random deterioration.

## Agency check

At major turns, ask:

1. What does the character want right now?
2. What action do they take rather than merely endure?
3. Why is the rejected alternative plausible?
4. What consequence belongs specifically to their choice?
5. How does that consequence alter their next decision?

If the plot would proceed identically with another character, increase role-specific knowledge, leverage, obligation, or vulnerability.

## Relationship state

Track relationships as changing systems, not labels. For each important pair record:

- public role versus private need;
- trust and what it is based on;
- power and which resource creates it;
- debt, obligation, resentment, attraction, fear, or dependency;
- information asymmetry;
- the last action that changed the relationship;
- the next unresolved test.

Every meaningful relationship scene should change at least one of: knowledge, trust, power, obligation, intimacy, or distance.

## Distinct dialogue identity

Differentiate characters with decision-relevant habits:

- what they say directly versus avoid;
- preferred vocabulary and level of precision;
- sentence completion, interruption, and question habits;
- metaphors grounded in their work or experience;
- tactics under pressure: joke, attack, bargain, lecture, deflect, comply;
- what makes their speech become shorter, formal, repetitive, or silent.

Do not rely only on accent, catchphrases, or misspelling.

## Character consistency

Consistency means action follows current desire, knowledge, belief, pressure, and history. It does not mean behavior never changes. A surprising action is earned when the project files contain the pressure and intermediate evidence that made it possible.
